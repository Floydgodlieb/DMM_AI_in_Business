# AI and the economy

Estimates of what AI does to the whole economy range from modest to large, depending on who models it and on what assumptions. None is a measurement of the reader's market.

## Cautious estimates

- An NBER working paper (Acemoglu) estimates no more than a 0.66% rise in total factor productivity over 10 years [W4-107], and under 0.53% on the paper's more cautious reading [W4-108]; it expects the gap between capital and labor income to widen [W4-109].
- An ECB speech finds little evidence so far of substantial employment effects [W4-112].

## Larger scenarios

- The ECB speech sees faster adoption yielding 0.3-0.4 percentage points more productivity growth per year over the coming decade, a scenario, not a measured result [W4-113].
- The WTO projects global trade up 34 to 37 per cent by 2040 [W4-077] and GDP up 12 to 13 per cent [W4-078].

## Exposure

- About 40 percent of global employment is exposed to AI [W4-183]; advanced economies carry more risk and are better placed to benefit [W4-183]. See [[ai-and-jobs]].

## Contradictions and tensions

> [!contradiction] W4-107 vs W4-078: at most 0.66% productivity over ten years vs 12 to 13 per cent more global GDP
> Different measures, horizons and assumptions: a one-time productivity (TFP) gain over ten years [W4-107] against WTO scenarios to 2040 for GDP [W4-078]. The WTO range comes from scenario modelling, not measurement [W4-077]; the NBER working paper builds on task-level exposure [W4-107]. Neither is evidence about a single trader.

> [!contradiction] W4-107 vs W4-113: under 0.66% in total over ten years vs 0.3-0.4 points a year
> Taken together: gains of 0.3-0.4 points a year [W4-113] would add up to more than the NBER ceiling [W4-107]. The ECB figure is conditional on faster adoption and on how much of GDP is exposed [W4-113].

## Gaps

- No claim connects macro estimates to margins or prices in used-vehicle and machinery trade.
