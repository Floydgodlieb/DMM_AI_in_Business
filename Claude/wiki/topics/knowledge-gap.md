# The knowledge gap

Every source that asks firms why they do not use AI puts lack of knowledge, expertise or skills near the top; small firms also lack the in-house IT people who could fill it. The newer sources add legal and privacy worries, and in some a sense that AI is not useful.

## What firms say holds them back

- EU-wide, lack of expertise is the top reason not to use AI (70.89%), ahead of legal uncertainty and privacy [W4-022].
- Dutch micro firms that decided against AI name lack of experience most (71.6%) [W4-023]; about half name privacy [W4-024]. CBS 2025 (provisional): 49 percent of businesses name privacy and 42 percent legal consequences [W4-159].
- In Dutch trade and logistics, almost two thirds name a lack of knowledge and skills [W4-014]; only seven percent have an AI policy [W4-013] and 42 percent have their data largely in order [W4-015].
- Dutch SME owners mainly struggle to understand AI and what it can do for their own firm [W4-027].
- OECD survey of non-adopters (self-reports): unsuitability to the firm's work 57%, copyright or legal worries 54%, worry about information fed into models 52%, lack of staff skills 50% [W4-176]. OECD paper: skill shortages are consistently among SMEs' main hurdles [W4-181].
- ECB survey of non-users: 30% see no usefulness; around 20% name incompatibility with existing systems and a skills shortage [W4-117].
- G7: lower adoption in manufacturing, transportation, construction and agriculture is put down to legacy systems, limited digital maturity and lack of internal AI expertise [W4-146].

## Research on SME barriers

- Pilot interviews in the Czech Republic and Austria: lack of trust, a knowledge gap and infrastructure limits [W4-151] [W4-152] [W4-153]; qualitative, small sample [W4-154].
- A preprint (not peer-reviewed, no data of its own): cost, lack of technical skills and employee acceptance [W4-160].
- Romanian SMEs (self-reported survey, small sample): a study of drivers and barriers [W4-148] [W4-147].

## No IT department

- In 2021 only 31 percent of Dutch firms with 10+ staff employed an ICT specialist [W4-025]; in 2022, 21 percent trained their own ICT specialists [W4-026].
- The research literature reports the same: scarce, costly skills [W4-057], few AI specialists [W4-064], poor expertise and management styles [W4-063], fear of cost [W4-065].
- Complex AI may not be feasible for small firms at all [W4-062]; complexity and cost explain why SMEs lag [W4-055].
- Small firms list money limits, lack of expertise, resistance to change and integration problems [W4-049].

## Contradictions and tensions

> [!contradiction] W4-023 vs W4-022: the Dutch figure is for micro firms, the EU figure for all firms
> Both put lack of expertise first by a wide margin, so the direction agrees; neither is for firms of the reader's size.

> [!contradiction] W4-158 vs W4-022: three quarters have not considered AI vs a list of reasons from firms that did
> The EU reasons come from firms that had considered using AI [W4-022]; CBS says most Dutch businesses have not considered it at all [W4-158]. The knowledge gap may be larger than the reason lists suggest, since firms that never looked are not asked.

> [!contradiction] W4-117 vs W4-022: ECB firms name lack of usefulness first; Eurostat firms name lack of expertise
> Different surveys, populations (euro area firms of all sizes, self-reported [W4-117]; EU firms that considered AI [W4-022]) and answer options. Neither is for the reader's size.
