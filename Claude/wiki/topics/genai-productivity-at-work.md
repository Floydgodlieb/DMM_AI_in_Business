# Generative AI and productivity at work

The scientific evidence layer: in controlled studies of single firms generative AI raises output noticeably, mostly for less-experienced people and only on tasks inside what the tool can do. None of the studies is of a trader of the reader's size; almost all are working papers or author drafts.

## Field experiments (large gains, large firms)

- Customer support (NBER working paper): productivity rose 14% on average and 34% for novice and low-skilled workers, with minimal impact on experienced workers [W4-126]; the agents worked for one Fortune 500 software firm [W4-127].
- Consultants (HBS working paper with BCG): on tasks inside the frontier of AI capability, 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, more than 40% higher quality [W4-164].
- Software developers (author draft, Microsoft-employed authors, Microsoft/GitHub tool): a 26.08% increase in completed tasks (SE: 10.3%) [W4-168]; less experienced developers gained more [W4-169]. The authors call the implementation ad hoc [W4-170].
- Online retail (preprint, not peer-reviewed, authors tied to the partner company): sales effects from no detectable impact to 16.3%, without worse returns or ratings [W4-087] [W4-088].

## Outside the frontier

- The same consultants were 19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions on a task outside the frontier [W4-165]. See [[ai-reliability-and-hallucination]].

## What happens in the wider workforce

- In a US survey (NBER working paper), 23 percent of employed respondents used generative AI for work in the previous week and 9 percent every work day [W4-133]; between 1 and 5 percent of work hours are AI-assisted and reported time savings equal 1.4 percent of hours [W4-134].
- In Danish records, chatbot users mostly (85%) move saved time to other tasks [W4-131], and earnings and hours show precise null effects, ruling out effects above 2% [W4-129].
- Firms that adopt AI see higher growth in revenue, measured productivity, profits and employment [W4-137].
- SMEs using generative AI (OECD survey, self-reported): 65% say it helped employee performance, only 26% say it raised revenue [W4-174].

## Contradictions and tensions

> [!contradiction] W4-126 / W4-168 vs W4-107: large task-level gains in field experiments vs a modest macro estimate
> The experiments measure single tasks in single large firms [W4-127] [W4-168]. The NBER macro paper estimates no more than 0.66% extra productivity over ten years [W4-107] and expects early evidence from easy tasks to overstate the future [W4-108]. Different level of measurement; taken together, task gains do not simply add up to the economy.

> [!contradiction] W4-126 vs W4-129: measured productivity gains vs no earnings or hours effect
> Gains per worker in an experiment [W4-126] sit beside precise null effects on Danish earnings and hours [W4-129]. Both are working papers; one is a single firm, the other a whole labour market two years after ChatGPT. Time saved may go into other tasks rather than pay [W4-131].

> [!contradiction] W4-087 vs W4-088: sales gains in "most" workflows vs some with no effect
> The retail preprint finds gains in most workflows but none detectable in others [W4-087]; its authors are tied to the partner company [W4-087].

## Gaps

- No claim covers productivity effects in a business-to-business trading firm or in a firm of the reader's size.
- Which tasks of the lead-to-export-document chain sit inside the frontier is not answered by any claim.
- Most claims are working papers; several are not peer-reviewed or have a conflict of interest, so the figures carry that label wherever used.
