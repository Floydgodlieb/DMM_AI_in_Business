## 1. The research problem

### What Draghi found

The report on European competitiveness of 9 September 2024 traces Europe's growth gap with the United States and China to technology, though not to any shortage of ideas. The problem is diffusion. European firms keep pouring research money into mature sectors while digital tools spread slowly through the businesses that already exist. Draghi lists the barriers plainly: a fragmented single market, regulatory load (55 percent of EU SMEs name administrative and regulatory burden as their biggest obstacle), thin access to capital, missing skills, too little scale. He treats reliance on high-risk suppliers outside Europe as a separate worry rather than the main one. That separate worry turns out to matter here, and section 2 gives it a question of its own.

### Where diffusion stalls

Diffusion is not evenly stalled, and the most recent Dutch measurement shows exactly where it sticks. CBS surveyed ICT use over reporting year 2025 and published the AI results on 12 December 2025; a follow-up report of 16 March 2026 broke the smallest firms out separately. Both are provisional figures, and criterion 4 makes the handbook say so.

| AI use in 2025 (CBS, provisional) | Share of firms |
|---|---|
| All firms, 2 or more staff | 17 percent |
| 2 to 10 staff | 14 percent |
| 10 to 50 staff | 27 percent |
| 50 to 250 staff | 45 percent |
| 250 or more staff | 66 percent |
| Trade | 12 percent |
| Transport and storage | 7 percent |

Two gradients in one table, pointing opposite ways. Size predicts adoption steeply: from 14 percent at the smallest firms to 66 percent at the largest. Sector predicts it the other way, and trade and transport sit at the bottom of the eleven branches CBS reports, alongside hospitality and construction. The Dutch trade in used commercial vehicles, trailers and machinery lands on the intersection: firms mostly in the 10 to 50 class, where 27 percent use something, inside a branch at 12 percent, selling into one at 7 percent.

What that AI is used for sharpens it further. Across all AI-using firms, marketing or sales is the most common purpose at 35 percent, and in trade it is close to half. Logistics is the least common purpose of any, at 4 percent. So the trade has not ignored AI. It has put it where customers can see it and nowhere near the chain that carries a vehicle from quotation to delivered export document. That is the gap this research is about.

The firms that stay out say why. Three quarters have not used AI and have not considered it. Among those who considered it and declined, the reasons given are lack of relevant experience (73 percent), privacy (49 percent) and unclear legal consequences (42 percent); only 15 percent say they do not find it useful. Cost and technology do not lead that list. What does lead it is knowledge, trust and legal uncertainty, which is the complexity question in the main question, stated by the sector about itself.

For scale outside the Netherlands: Eurostat puts EU enterprises with 10 or more staff at 20.0 percent in 2025, up from 13.5 percent in 2024, with large firms around 55 percent against 19 percent for SMEs. The Netherlands sits in the European leading group. Draghi's diffusion gap is therefore not a Dutch problem being solved; it is a size-and-sector problem that looks the same across the union.

*A caution the chapter carries.* CBS reports two different size cuts. The December 2025 release counts from 2 staff upward; the March 2026 report uses 2 to 9, 10 to 249 and 250-plus, giving 13.8, 29.8 and 66.2 percent. The 66 percent figure appears in both and the rest do not line up. Figures get compared inside one cut, never across the two.

### Why this trade, and why a case

The interesting question in this trade is not whether AI could help. It is how much complexity a firm here can carry before the tooling stops being worth its trouble. Gibson's line that *the future is already here, it is just not evenly distributed* is the working assumption: somewhere in this trade the harder steps are already automated, and the research is a search for where, followed by a judgement about how far down the size distribution that reaches.

BAS World is where we look for the already-distributed part. More than 450 staff, an in-house portal, an AI Search running in production, a multilingual chat and flowbot layer, a staffed export-documentation function: it is the frontier of this trade rather than a typical member of it. That is exactly what makes it useful as a case. It shows what the process looks like when money and IT capacity are not the limit, which isolates what *is* the limit. It cannot show what the sector does on average, and section 5 says what we put in its place.

> "Where has this actually worked in our trade, and how much of it could a firm my size run?"

*— The question the handbook has to answer, in a trader's words*

## 2. Research question and sub-questions

**Main question.** How far can AI responsibly reach into the international, multilingual sales and customer processes of the Dutch trade in used commercial vehicles and machinery, and what level of complexity can firms in that sector actually carry?

The sub-questions sit in two sets. The external set asks what the environment around these firms is doing and demanding of them. The internal set asks what a firm in the trade is, can afford, and will accept. BAS World supplies the deep evidence for the internal set and one data point for the external one.

### External analysis

| # | Angle | Sub-question |
|---|---|---|
| **E1** | Competition and best practice | Which steps in this trade's chain already run on AI somewhere, and which run on AI nowhere? |
| **E2** | Autonomy and dependency | On which suppliers, established and hosted where, does this trade's AI tooling depend? |
| **E3** | Rules and exposure | Which legal duties apply to AI in cross-border trade in used vehicles, for the tooling as well as the goods? |

### Internal analysis

| # | Angle | Sub-question |
|---|---|---|
| **I1** | Current state | Which steps between a lead and a delivered export document are automated in firms in this trade today? |
| **I2** | Capacity to absorb | What money and which roles does a firm in this trade need to run one more automated step? |
| **I3** | Boundary | Where do staff draw the line past which automation would cost customer trust, once they know what their tooling actually does? |

**How the six fit together.** E1 to E3 give the benchmark, the dependency and the binding duties. I1 to I3 give the starting point, the capacity to move from it, and the limit the trade's own people accept. Together they answer the main question: what level of complexity this sector can carry. Drop I1 and we recommend what already exists. Drop I2 and we recommend what nobody is staffed for. Drop I3 and we recommend what nobody will use.

## 3. Scope and case selection

| Dimension | Description |
|---|---|
| **Sector** | International B2B trade in used commercial vehicles, trailers and machinery, sold in many languages into mostly non-EU markets. Sales, documentation and after-sales processes only. |
| **Region** | The Netherlands as the trading hub, selling into more than 150 countries. Noord-Brabant and Gelderland carry a large share of the trade. |
| **Case** | BAS World, Veghel, part of family-owned BAS Group. Selected on purpose as a frontier case, not as a representative one. |
| **Who the handbook serves** | The owner or commercial manager of a Dutch trader in this business with roughly 10 to 50 staff, no IT function, and the same export documentation and screening duties as the largest player in the trade. |
| **Secondary reader** | Commercial and operations management at BAS World, who host the case and get the findings back. |

## 4. Quality criteria for the handbook

**Floor criteria from the chapter template.** Clear to a non-technical reader. Accurate against appraised sources. Covers responsible AI. Useful to an owner.

### Ours on top of those

| # | Criterion |
|---|---|
| 1 | Every recommendation attaches to a **named step** in the trade's sales or documentation chain, never to a category of tool. |
| 2 | Time-saving automation and automation that touches the customer relationship stay apart, and each recommendation says which of the two it is. |
| 3 | Each recommendation names the **duty that applies** (AI Act, GDPR, sanctions screening) and what evidence would show compliance. Where it depends on an outside supplier, it also names where that supplier hosts and processes. |
| 4 | Every figure carries two labels: company-reported or independently verifiable, and **case evidence or sector evidence**. A finding that rests on BAS World alone says so in the sentence that makes it, never in a footnote. |
| 5 | Every recommendation carries the **level of complexity** it needs: *runs on bought tooling*, with no process owner and no IT function; *needs a process owner*, somebody whose job it is to keep it honest; or *needs in-house build*, which usually means data volume or staff a small trader does not have. The third label is a warning, and the chapter says what to do instead. |
| 6 | The page closes with one concrete next step: an owner, a rough cost, and a way to tell inside three months whether it worked. |

## 5. Methodology

The sector claim and the case claim are gathered separately and stay separately labelled. Three strands feed them.

### Strand 1: desk research on the sector

| Element | How we handle it |
|---|---|
| **Sources** | EU primary material (consolidated AI Act text, Regulation 833/2014 with its amending packages, OLAF publications), Dutch statistical and registry material (CBS, KvK), the Draghi report, sector and branch bodies, trade press. For E1 we add a second set outside the peer group: export documentation, customs and trade-compliance vendors serving forwarders and traders, since that is the layer the peer platforms do not reach. |
| **Supplier research for E2** | Country of establishment, hosting region, sub-processor list and data-transfer basis, read from the supplier's own contractual documentation rather than its marketing pages. A supplier's description of its own compliance counts as a claim by that supplier. Where documentation is missing, the gap itself gets recorded, because a dependency nobody can describe is the finding. |
| **Appraisal** | Each source scored on credibility (who publishes it, what interest they hold), relevance (which sub-question it feeds) and recency (regulation dated to the version in force in September 2026). A company's own description of itself counts as a claim about the company, never as independent evidence. Where sources disagree we report the disagreement instead of picking the bigger number. |
| **Language** | Dutch sources get appraised in Dutch, before any translation, and are translated only for drafting. PRD section 6 covers the mechanics. |
| **Feeds** | E1, E2, E3. |

### Strand 2: sector-side scan of smaller traders

| Element | How we handle it |
|---|---|
| **What** | A structured scan of 20 to 30 Dutch traders in the same business, drawn from KvK registration and the peer platforms' seller listings, filtered to roughly 10 to 50 staff. |
| **What we record** | Per firm and per process step: whether an AI or automated feature is visible on their own channels (search, matching, multilingual first contact, quotation, documentation), and which supplier it appears to come from where that is identifiable. |
| **Why** | Without it, every sector-level sentence in the handbook rests on one large company, and the difference between "this trade" and "this one firm" collapses. The scan is thin evidence, visible surface only, and it gets labelled as such under criterion 4. What it does carry is a distribution: how common is anything at all at this size. |
| **Feeds** | E1 and I1, and it is what makes the main question answerable at sector level. |

### Strand 3: case study at BAS World, one visit

| Element | How we handle it |
|---|---|
| **Who** | One or two people from international sales or customer service, and where we can get it, one from export documentation, since that is where sanctions and customs work lands. For I2 we also need whoever signs off on tooling spend, even briefly. |
| **Subject** | A walk through the chain from lead to delivered document, with questions per step on tooling, time spent, language and irritation, followed by the boundary question from I3. |
| **Order of questioning** | Belief first, then boundary, then correction. Each respondent describes what they think the AI Search and the chat layer already do, in their own words, before the interviewer says anything about how those work. Only then comes the boundary question. Afterwards the interviewer states what the tooling verifiably does and asks the boundary question again. Whether the line moves is recorded. |
| **Why that order** | A boundary answer is worth nothing without the belief behind it. Someone who thinks the AI Search only matches keywords and someone who thinks it reads a customer's intent can give the same answer for opposite reasons, and a recommendation built on that answer will fail for one of them. Ask the boundary question first and the interviewer's own framing supplies the belief, which is exactly what we are trying to measure. The size and direction of the gap is also evidence for the AI literacy duty in Article 4. |
| **Recording and handling** | Recorded only with consent given beforehand. The transcript stays inside the team. People are anonymised by role rather than by name. The recording goes to no service outside the environment the team has agreed on. Everything is deleted once the module ends. |
| **Feeds** | I1, I2, I3, and the frontier floor described in section 3. |

### What this design cannot tell us

One case is one case. Nothing from the visit generalises to the sector on its own, and the scan in strand 2 sees only what firms put on their own channels, which understates internal tooling and overstates nothing. What staff report about their own time and irritation is self-report, and the correction step improves the boundary answer without turning it into a measurement. E2 depends on supplier documentation that suppliers are not obliged to publish. We report these limits in the chapter rather than writing around them, and criterion 4 makes every sentence say which strand it came from.

> **The bar.** A reader can tell which sector the handbook serves, what it has to answer, which evidence is the sector's and which is one company's, and how we will judge whether it is any good.
