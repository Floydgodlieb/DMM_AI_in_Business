**Research Proposal**

**AI in international, multilingual sales and customer processes in the Dutch trade in used commercial vehicles and machinery, with BAS World as case**

Aäron Bos · Floyd Godlieb · Ivan van Vreeswijk · Baran Yapici | v2 | 17 September 2026

**1\. The research problem**

Draghi's 2024 report blames Europe's growth gap on slow diffusion of existing technology, not a lack of ideas, worsened by market fragmentation, heavy regulation, thin capital access, missing skills and too little scale.

| **AI use in 2025 (CBS, provisional)** | **Share of firms** |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| 2 to 10 staff                         | 14 percent         |
| 10 to 50 staff                        | 27 percent         |
| 50 to 250 staff                       | 45 percent         |
| 250 or more staff                     | 66 percent         |
| Trade                                 | 12 percent         |
| Transport and storage                 | 7 percent          |

Adoption rises steeply with firm size, but trade and transport sit near the bottom of CBS's eleven branches, the opposite direction.

The Dutch trade in used commercial vehicles, trailers and machinery sits on that intersection: mostly firms with 10 to 50 staff, inside a 12 percent branch that sells into a 7 percent one. Where AI is used, it clusters in marketing and sales (35 percent) and almost never in logistics (4 percent), exactly the chain from quotation to export document. Firms that declined AI cite a lack of experience (73 percent), privacy (49 percent) and legal uncertainty (42 percent), rarely cost. Eurostat shows the same pattern EU-wide, so this is not a uniquely Dutch problem.

The real question is not whether AI could help, but how much complexity a firm here can carry. BAS World, more than 450 staff with an AI Search already in production, is the frontier of this trade rather than a typical member of it: that is what makes it useful for isolating what stays hard once budget and IT are no longer the limit. It cannot describe the sector on average, so section 5 covers that separately.

**2\. Research question and sub-questions**

**Main question** How far can AI responsibly reach into the international, multilingual sales and customer processes of the Dutch trade in used commercial vehicles and machinery, and what level of complexity can firms in that sector actually carry?

The external sub-questions ask what the environment demands; the internal ones ask what a firm in the trade is, can afford and will accept. BAS World supplies detailed evidence for the internal set and one data point for the external one.

| **Set**  | **#** | **Angle**                     | **Sub-question**                                                       |
| -------- | ----- | ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| External | E1    | Competition and best practice | Which steps already run on AI somewhere, and which run on AI nowhere?  |
| External | E2    | Autonomy and dependency       | Which suppliers, hosted where, does this trade's AI tooling depend on? |
| External | E3    | Rules and exposure            | Which legal duties apply to AI in cross-border trade in used vehicles? |
| Internal | I1    | Current state                 | Which steps from lead to export document are automated today?          |
| Internal | I2    | Capacity to absorb            | What money and roles does one more automated step need?                |
| Internal | I3    | Boundary                      | Where do staff draw the line on automation that costs customer trust?  |

Together they answer the main question. Drop I1 and we recommend what already exists; drop I2, what nobody can staff; drop I3, what nobody will use.

**3\. Scope and case selection**

| **Sector**           | Used commercial vehicles, trailers and machinery; B2B, multilingual, mostly non-EU markets.                     |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Region**           | The Netherlands as trading hub, selling into more than 150 countries, mainly from Noord-Brabant and Gelderland. |
| **Case**             | BAS World, Veghel, part of BAS Group: a frontier case rather than a representative one.                         |
| **Primary reader**   | Owner or commercial manager of a Dutch trader with 10 to 50 staff and no IT function.                           |
| **Secondary reader** | BAS World's commercial and operations management, who host the case.                                            |

A frontier case shows what stays hard even without budget or IT limits, a usable floor for the sector. It cannot describe the sector on its own, so section 5 covers that separately.

**4\. Quality criteria for the handbook**

Floor criteria from the chapter template: clear to a non-technical reader, accurate, responsible, useful to an owner. We add six of our own:

| **#** | **Criterion**                                                                                                                      |
| ----- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Names a step in the sales or documentation chain, never a category of tool.                                                        |
| 2     | Time-saving automation is kept apart from automation that touches the customer, and each recommendation says which.                |
| 3     | Names the duty that applies (AI Act, GDPR, sanctions screening), the evidence for it, and any outside supplier's hosting location. |
| 4     | Every figure is labelled company-reported or independently verifiable, and case evidence or sector evidence.                       |
| 5     | Every recommendation is labelled by complexity: bought tooling, a named process owner, or an in-house build, flagged as a warning. |
| 6     | Closes with one next step: an owner, a rough cost, and a three-month check.                                                        |

**5\. Methodology**

Three strands keep the sector claim and the case claim separate.

**Strand 1: desk research on the sector**

- Sources: the AI Act, GDPR, Regulation 833/2014, CBS, KvK, Draghi, trade press, plus export and compliance vendors for E1.
- Method: read suppliers' own contractual documentation, not marketing pages; score sources on credibility, relevance and recency; report disagreement rather than pick the bigger number. Dutch sources are appraised in Dutch first (PRD section 5).
- Feeds: E1, E2, E3.

**Strand 2: a sector-side scan of smaller traders**

- Scan 20 to 30 Dutch traders (KvK and peer platforms, 10 to 50 staff) for AI features visible on their own channels, per process step.
- Why: keeps sector-level claims from resting on one company. Thin, visible-surface evidence only (criterion 4).
- Feeds: E1, I1, and the main question at sector level.

**Strand 3: a case study at BAS World, one visit**

- Who: one or two sales or customer-service staff, ideally someone from export documentation, and briefly whoever signs off on tooling spend.
- Method: walk the chain from lead to document; ask the boundary question belief first, then again after explaining the tooling, and record whether the answer moved.
- Recording: consent given beforehand, anonymised by role, deleted after the module. Processing environment still to be confirmed (PRD section 8).
- Feeds: I1, I2, I3, and the frontier floor from section 3.

One case does not generalise. The scan shows only visible-surface tooling, and self-reported time is not a real measurement. We flag these limits and state which strand every claim came from.

**The bar** A reader can tell which sector this serves, what it must answer, whose evidence is whose, and how we will judge whether it is any good.
