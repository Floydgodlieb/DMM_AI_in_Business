# Assignment 1 — Report writing with AI

Aäron Bos · AIBS/AEL minor, "AI in Business" · 17 September 2026

**Method note.** The three pieces below were produced in three separate, uncoordinated prompts to the same assistant: "Write a confident, well-argued article on why [the United States / China / Europe] leads the global AI race." No prompt mentioned the other two. Nothing was cross-checked while writing. They are kept exactly as generated — including their thin sourcing — because that thinness is the point of the exercise. They are evidence for assignment 2 and for the handbook page: the shape a confident, one-sided AI answer takes. They are not used as sources anywhere else in this hand-in.

## Article 1 — Why the United States leads the global AI race

The numbers settle this faster than the debate suggests. In 2025, private investment in American AI companies reached roughly $286 billion — nearly 23 times China's figure for the same year — and five U.S. hyperscalers now control more than two-thirds of the world's AI computing capacity. Frontier labs headquartered in the US have raised over $370 billion in total, a scale of capital no other bloc comes close to matching.

That money buys talent and iteration speed. OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind between them produced the large majority of the world's most-cited "notable" AI models in 2024, and OpenAI's revenue has been compounding at roughly 3x a year since 2024 — proof, if any were needed, that the market is rewarding the American approach at a speed no competitor can match. Where China ships a clever, cheap model every few months, the US ships an entire ecosystem: chips (Nvidia), cloud (Microsoft, Amazon, Google), model labs, and the enterprise software that turns a model into a product.

Europe, meanwhile, is not really in this race. It produced a handful of notable models in 2024 against America's dozens, and its flagship response — regulation — does not build a single chip or train a single model. Rules do not compound the way capital and compute do.

None of this is close. The US set the terms of the current AI era with GPT-4 and has extended its lead with every funding round since. Betting against American AI leadership in this decade would require ignoring where the money, the chips, and the frontier labs actually are.

*(Word count: ~280. No sources cited in the generated text; figures are asserted, not attributed.)*

## Article 2 — Why China leads the global AI race

Money is not the scoreboard — capability per dollar is, and on that measure China is pulling ahead. DeepSeek trained a model competitive with 2024's best American systems for a reported $5.6 million, using a fraction of the compute a US lab would consider necessary. Despite spending roughly 23 times less than the United States on AI in 2025, China's leading models trailed the best American ones by only a few percentage points on public benchmarks by early 2026 — a gap that keeps shrinking even as the spending gap stays enormous.

Washington's own actions prove the point. Years of export controls on advanced chips were meant to keep China dependent on American hardware. Instead, they forced Huawei and domestic foundries to build an independent chip supply chain, and today Chinese AI companies increasingly run on Chinese silicon by choice as much as by necessity. A strategy that drives your rival to self-sufficiency has not contained them — it has freed them.

China also wins where it matters commercially: deployment. Chinese AI models, many released as open weights, are being adopted across manufacturing, logistics, and government systems at a pace Western firms — slowed by procurement cycles, compliance reviews, and boardroom caution — cannot match. Efficiency, state-backed scale, and a willingness to ship into the real economy immediately are a more durable advantage than a bigger training budget.

The United States still spends more. China is closing the gap on far less, building the hardware independence that removes its one real vulnerability, and getting more of its AI into actual use. That is not a country losing an AI race. That is a country running a smarter one.

## Article 3 — Why Europe leads the global AI race

Leadership in AI is not only about who trains the biggest model first — it is about who sets the terms everyone else eventually has to follow, and who builds AI that businesses and governments can actually trust and legally deploy. On both counts, Europe is out in front.

The EU AI Act is the world's first comprehensive, binding AI law, and its transparency rules are already in force. Whatever a company builds in San Francisco or Shenzhen, if it wants to operate in the EU's half-a-billion-person single market, it now has to meet European standards on disclosure, oversight, and risk management. That is the "Brussels effect" that already reshaped global data protection through GDPR, and it is repeating itself with AI: rules written in Brussels become the default rules multinational companies build to everywhere.

Europe is backing that regulatory leadership with real capital, not just paperwork. The EU's AI Continent Action Plan targets €200 billion in combined public and private investment, including new AI "gigafactories" across member states. And European industry is no longer just a rule-taker: Mistral AI's €3 billion funding round in September 2026, valuing the company at roughly €21 billion, shows that Europe can build a genuine frontier competitor — one explicitly positioned as a trustworthy, sovereign alternative to US labs for companies and governments that don't want their AI infrastructure controlled from outside Europe.

Raw compute and training budgets are a snapshot. Rules, trust, and sovereignty are durable advantages that outlast any single funding round or model release. The US and China are racing to build the most powerful model. Europe is building the framework — legal, ethical, and industrial — that determines who gets to use any of it, and on whose terms. That is the harder, and ultimately more consequential, race to lead.

## What three uncoordinated answers show, read back to back

**What they agree on.** All three treat "AI leadership" as a single, knowable contest with one right answer, all reach confidently for 2025–2026 data points, and all reference the same handful of touchstones (DeepSeek, Mistral, hyperscaler compute, the AI Act) — just to opposite conclusions.

**Where they contradict.** Each one wins by silently changing what "leads" means: Article 1 measures capital and compute, Article 2 measures capability-per-dollar and deployment speed, Article 3 measures rule-setting and trust. None of the three states that it has picked a different scoreboard from the others, so the three "the leader is X" claims are not actually disagreeing about the same question — they only look like they are.

**What none of them cites.** Not one names a dataset, a named author, a publication date, or a methodology behind a single figure — the $286bn, the $5.6m, the €200bn all appear as bare assertions. None discloses whose interest sits behind the framing it reaches for (a US-capital-markets view, a techno-nationalist efficiency view, a Brussels policy view). And none flags that a lead on one layer (say, US investment) says nothing about what a Dutch SME actually depends on: which vendor, hosted where, under whose law.

**The bar.** Three separate, single-shot answers to "who leads AI" produced three internally consistent, well-written, and irreconcilable stories — because each one is answering a different, unstated question with real but selectively chosen numbers. That is why "ask AI who leads" is not a research method: the assistant does not fail by getting facts wrong, it fails by never stating its own scope, and a reader cannot tell that from the confident tone alone. Assignment 2 sets out the criteria and strategy needed to actually answer the question this exercise couldn't.
